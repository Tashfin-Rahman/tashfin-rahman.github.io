Keeping the books for a shop and for a project business

October 2026 · Tashfin Rahman, Home Inn

I have done both. For two and a half years I kept the daily books for an online store selling digital products. Now I keep them for a firm that designs and builds interiors and furniture, where one job can run for months. Same double entry, same spreadsheets, same closing at the end of the month. Very little else carries across.

I found that out by carrying habits from the first job into the second, so this is written from the mistake rather than from a textbook.

A shop's problem is volume

Many small transactions, every day, and nearly all of them are fine. The real work is matching: a payment lands, and it has to be attached to the order it belongs to. One payment that cannot be matched is nothing. Forty of them is a day gone.

So you reconcile daily, and not out of discipline for its own sake. A week of unmatched payments takes far longer to clear than seven days of a few minutes each, because by then you have lost the context. You no longer remember which customer wrote in about a duplicate charge, or which order was cancelled and refunded. The ledger keeps the amounts and forgets everything around them.

The other thing volume gives you is a baseline. When you see the same shape of day for a few weeks, an odd one stands out on its own without anybody looking for it. That is most of what watching the daily transactions amounts to.

A project business's problem is timing

Fewer transactions, much larger, spread across months. The matching is easy. The timing is not.

Money leaves early and arrives late. Materials are bought and workshop labour is paid while the job is being built. The client pays at milestones, or at handover. A job can be perfectly profitable and still leave the firm short in the middle of it, and the profit figure will not warn you, because profit and cash are answering different questions.

This is why the costing has to be per job rather than per month. A monthly statement for a firm with four jobs at four different stages is an average of things that have nothing to do with each other. It tells you the firm is roughly fine. It does not tell you that one job is running over its materials estimate while there is still time to do something about it.

What the partners actually read

Not the ledger. At Home Inn I prepare the summaries the partners plan against, and the ones that get used are short and answer the decision in front of them. Where each live job stands against what it was costed at. What is going out in the next few weeks and what is due in. What a new commitment would do to that.

A long report gets read once. A short one gets read every week, which is the only version that changes a decision.

The habit I had to drop

The monthly rhythm. For a shop a month is a natural unit: enough transactions for the comparison to mean something, and it lines up with how costs arrive. For a project business it is arbitrary. A job does not know when the month ends. Closing the books on the last day of October tells you where the calendar is, not where the work is.

What replaced it was closing against the job. Each one gets looked at when it reaches a stage, and the month is just when the tax and the payroll happen.

If you run a small business and your accounts only ever report by month, that is the thing worth checking first. Not whether the numbers are right, but whether they are cut the way the business actually works.

Tashfin Rahman is Partner and Chief Business Officer at Home Inn, which has designed and built architecture, interiors and custom furniture across Bangladesh since 2015. He was previously Senior Accountant at an online store.
