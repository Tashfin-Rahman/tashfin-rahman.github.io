What you are actually paying for in a fit-out quote

October 2026 · Tashfin Rahman, Home Inn

Most people ask for a price before anyone has seen the room. That is fair enough. You want to know whether this is affordable before you spend an afternoon on it. But a number given at that stage is a guess, and a guess has to be padded to be safe, so you end up hearing a figure larger than the work would actually cost.

Here is how a quote gets built where I work, and what moves it.

It starts with the room, not a price list

We visit, measure, and ask what the space is for. This matters more than it sounds. A wardrobe built for a room with a low ceiling is a different piece of work from the same wardrobe with another foot of height, even though both are “a wardrobe” on a price list. One needs a different carcass, different hinges, and a different way of getting it through the door.

Catalogue furniture solves this by making you fit the room to the product. Made-to-order solves it the other way round, which is why it has to be costed before it can be priced.

What goes into the number

Every job breaks into roughly the same parts:

  • Materials. Board, timber, laminate, hardware, glass, paint. This is the part clients expect, and it is rarely the part that moves a quote.
  • Workshop labour. Cutting, assembly, finishing. We build in our own workshop rather than subcontracting, so this is a cost I can see and control rather than a margin someone else is taking.
  • Site work. Delivery, carrying, installation, and making good afterwards. A third floor with no lift is a different job from a ground floor with one.
  • Wastage. You never cut a full sheet without something left over, and the offcut is part of the cost whether or not anyone mentions it.
  • Contingency. Every job meets something it did not expect. That belongs inside the number, not arriving as a surprise at the end.

Where quotes go wrong

Three things move a number after it has been agreed, and all three are avoidable.

Changes after work starts. A client adds two drawers, then asks for a different handle, then wants the unit a little wider. Each change is small. Together they are not. We price changes as they come rather than at the end, so nobody is surprised at handover.

Site surprises. A wall that is not square, a floor that slopes, a beam nobody mentioned. These are normal, and a survey catches most of them. The ones it misses are what the contingency is for.

Material prices moving. Between quoting and building, prices move. A quote therefore has a life, and it is worth asking how long yours is good for rather than assuming.

What a client can do

Decide the finish early. Finish choices move a quote more than dimensions do, and changing your mind about a laminate after the carcass is cut means paying for both.

Ask for the breakdown. A firm that cannot show you where the money goes is either guessing or hiding something. We write it out, because a number you can see is easier to defend than one you cannot.

And let the measurement happen before you ask for the price. It costs you an afternoon, and it saves you the padding.

If you would rather see this than read it, I built a model you can move. Change the finish, the access or the number of changes after cutting starts, and watch which ones actually shift the number.
